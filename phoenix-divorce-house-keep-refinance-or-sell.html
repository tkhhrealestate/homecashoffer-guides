The Phoenix house in a divorce: deciding who keeps it, who refinances, or whether to sell

Most couples in a Phoenix divorce don't argue about whether the house matters. They argue about what to do with it. One spouse wants to stay in the Ahwatukee stucco two-story because the kids are at school down the street. The other has already signed a lease in Tempe and wants the equity out. The mortgage is in both names, the pool pump is making a new noise, and nobody wants to be the one paying a July electric bill on a house they no longer sleep in. This guide walks through the three realistic outcomes: one spouse keeps it, the house is sold, or the two of you hold it together for a while. It also covers the Arizona rules that shape each one.

Start with what Arizona already decided for you

Arizona is a community property state. Under A.R.S. 25-211, property either spouse acquires during the marriage is generally community property, with exceptions for gifts, inheritances, and property acquired after a dissolution petition is served (when the case ends in a decree). At the end of the case, A.R.S. 25-318 directs the court to divide community property equitably, which in practice usually starts from an equal split.

Two other rules matter for the house before anyone signs anything:

  • The preliminary injunction. When a petition is filed, A.R.S. 25-315 puts an injunction in place that generally bars either spouse from selling, transferring, or encumbering community property without the other's written consent or the court's permission. A listing agreement signed by only one spouse after filing is the kind of thing that ends up in front of a judge.
  • Joinder. A.R.S. 25-214 generally requires both spouses to join in any sale or encumbrance of community real property. Title companies in Maricopa County will usually ask for both signatures, or a court order, even when only one name is on the deed.

Before you weigh the options, pull the recorded deed from the Maricopa County Recorder and the latest mortgage statement. Write down whose names are on the deed, whose names are on the loan, the current payoff, and whether there is a HELOC or solar lease. Those four facts decide what each path costs.

Option 1: one spouse keeps the house

This sounds simple, and it is the option that most often falls apart late. The spouse leaving usually signs a deed, often a disclaimer or quitclaim deed, giving up their interest. That deed does nothing to the mortgage. The lender is not a party to your decree, and both of you stay liable on the note until the loan is refinanced or paid off. If the spouse who stays misses payments two years from now, it shows up on the other spouse's credit.

So "keeping the house" usually means three things have to happen together:

  • The staying spouse qualifies for a refinance on one income, at today's rates, for enough to pay off the existing loan and buy out the other spouse's share of equity.
  • The decree or a settlement agreement sets a deadline for that refinance and says what happens if it doesn't close. A common fallback is that the house gets listed or sold.
  • The deed from the departing spouse is signed and recorded at the refinance closing, not months before it, so nobody gives up title while still on the loan.

Get a refinance pre-approval in writing before you agree to this in mediation. A buyout number that looks fair on paper doesn't help if no lender will fund it.

Option 2: sell and split the proceeds

Selling is the cleanest break, and it is the one where timing matters most. Arizona has no state real estate transfer tax, so the seller's closing costs are mainly the loan payoff, title and escrow fees, prorated property taxes, any commissions and concessions, and an HOA payoff or transfer fee if you are in a planned community. Maricopa County property taxes are billed in two halves. The first half becomes delinquent after November 1 and the second after May 1, so escrow will prorate whichever half covers your closing date.

Things to settle in writing with your attorneys before you sign a purchase contract:

  • Who picks the price and approves offers, and what happens if you disagree.
  • Who pays the mortgage, utilities, HOA, and pool service until closing, and whether those payments get credited back out of the proceeds.
  • Whether net proceeds go straight to each spouse, or into an attorney trust account until the decree divides them.
  • Who handles repairs a buyer asks for, and how much either of you can approve without the other.

Option 3: hold the house together for now

Some couples agree to keep the house jointly until a youngest child finishes school, or until the market feels better. That can work, but treat it like a business arrangement. Your decree or agreement should spell out who lives there, who pays which bills, who covers a failed AC compressor in August, when the house will be sold, and how the eventual proceeds will be split, including credit for payments one spouse made alone. Without that, a deferred sale tends to turn into a second round of litigation.

A vacant house in the Valley is its own problem. Turning off the AC in summer can damage finishes and invite pests, and many insurance policies limit coverage after a house has been unoccupied for a set period. If neither of you is living there, ask the insurer what the policy says about vacancy before the house sits empty for months.

Listing versus a cash sale when you are separating

A traditional listing usually brings the higher price when the house shows well, both of you can cooperate on showings and repair requests, and there is time on the calendar. That includes buyer financing, an appraisal, and inspection negotiations, each of which is another decision two people in a divorce have to agree on.

A direct cash sale tends to fit when the house needs work neither spouse will pay for, one of you has already moved out, the carrying costs are coming out of two separate budgets, or a court date makes a predictable closing worth more than the last few dollars. You get one written number, no showings, and a closing date you can share with both attorneys. It usually trades away some retail upside, so put both numbers next to each other before deciding.
