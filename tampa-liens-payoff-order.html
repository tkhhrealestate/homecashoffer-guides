Selling a Tampa house with back taxes or liens: who gets paid first at closing

A lot of Tampa owners assume a lien means they can't sell. Usually it means the opposite: the sale is how the lien gets paid. What matters is how many claims are on the title, what order they get paid in, and whether there's anything left for you after the last check is cut.

Here's how the common ones work in Hillsborough County, roughly in the order a title company deals with them.

Unpaid property taxes and tax certificates

Florida property taxes become delinquent if they aren't paid by April 1. After that, the Hillsborough County Tax Collector sells a tax certificate on the unpaid amount at its annual sale. An investor pays the tax and earns interest from you; that rate is bid down at the auction from a statutory maximum of 18%.

The clock that matters: once two years have passed from April 1 of the year the certificate was issued, the holder can apply for a tax deed under Florida Statutes §197.502, which can end with the house going to public auction. If you have more than one year of certificates outstanding, look them up on the Tax Collector's site and get a redemption amount before you talk to any buyer.

At closing, redemption is straightforward. The title company pays the Tax Collector, the certificate is cancelled, and that money comes off the top of your proceeds.

Mortgage payoffs and HOA or condo estoppels

Next come recorded mortgages and any home equity line, each with a written payoff letter. If the house is in an HOA or condo association, Florida law requires the association to provide an estoppel certificate showing what's owed, including assessments, late fees, and legal charges. Associations in Florida can file liens for unpaid assessments, so a few months of skipped dues on a Westchase or New Tampa townhome turns into a line on the settlement statement.

City code enforcement liens

Overgrown yards, unpermitted additions, or a roof left under tarp can lead to code enforcement fines that keep running daily until the violation is fixed and then get recorded as a lien. These are the ones sellers miss, because they're recorded by the city, not sent by a lender.

The City of Tampa and Hillsborough County each have processes to request a lien search and, in some cases, ask for a reduction once the violation is cured. Don't assume the recorded amount is final. Do assume a buyer's title company will find it.

Judgment liens and the homestead question

A creditor who wins a court judgment can record it in the county's official records and attach it to real estate you own there. Florida's constitution gives strong protection to a homestead against most of those creditors, but that protection doesn't always make the recorded judgment disappear from the title search. Title companies often still want a release or a homestead affidavit. Rental or investment houses don't get that protection, so a judgment there usually gets paid from proceeds.

Doing the math before you pick a buyer

Write down every claim with a current payoff, then subtract that total from each offer, along with commissions, repairs, and closing costs. That gives you the number you'll actually walk away with. Some Tampa owners find a full listing still wins. Others find the repair list, the agent fee, and three more months of certificate interest erase the difference.
