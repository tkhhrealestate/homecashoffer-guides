A Notice of Default arrived on your Sacramento house. What to do before the trustee sale

The envelope is usually thicker than a regular mortgage statement, and it comes from a trustee company you may not recognize. For a lot of owners in Del Paso Heights, Meadowview, North Highlands or a 1950s tract in Arden-Arcade, that is the first clear sign that a missed payment has become a recorded Notice of Default. California does not take the house the day that notice is recorded. It does start a clock, and the useful work in the next week is gathering the right papers and deciding whether you are trying to keep the house or sell it in time.

First, read the recorded notice, not just the cover letter

Most California home loans are secured by a deed of trust with a power of sale, which is why the statutory notice says the property may be sold without a court case. The date that matters is the recording date with the Sacramento County Clerk-Recorder, not the day the letter reached your mailbox. You can look the document up in the county's public records by your name or the assessor's parcel number. Write down:

  • The recording date of the Notice of Default.
  • The trustee's name, phone number, and any sale-information line listed on the notice.
  • The loan number and the amount the notice says is past due, plus any costs it itemizes.
  • Whether a Notice of Trustee's Sale has already been recorded. If it has, the sale date on that notice is the one to plan around, including any later postponement.

Under Civil Code section 2924c, a Notice of Sale generally cannot be given until at least three months after the Notice of Default is recorded. After that, the sale date is the one printed on the most recent notice. Postponements happen, but they are not something to count on.

Ask for two different numbers: reinstatement and payoff

These get mixed up, and mixing them up is how people miss a deadline. Reinstatement is what it takes to bring the loan current and stop the foreclosure while you keep the house. Payoff is what it takes to pay the loan in full, which is what a sale has to cover.

Civil Code section 2924c(e) generally lets you reinstate by paying the past-due amount plus allowed costs until five business days before the scheduled sale. If the sale is postponed by more than five business days, that right usually comes back until five business days before the new date. After the window closes, stopping the sale on your own typically means paying the entire balance. Ask the servicer in writing for an itemized reinstatement quote and a separate payoff statement. The notice itself says they have to provide a reinstatement figure on written request.

If you still have a realistic path to catch up, talk to a HUD-certified housing counselor or a California attorney before you sign anything to sell. Keeping the house, when you can actually afford to, is usually the better outcome. If you are going to sell, the closing has to beat the sale date

The same statutory notice tells you that you may offer the property for sale as long as the sale is concluded before the foreclosure is. In Sacramento that usually means opening escrow with a title company that can see the recorded default, ordering the full payoff, and checking for anything else that will come out of the proceeds: a HELOC, a solar or property-assessed lien, unpaid Sacramento County taxes, or a city code lien.

Two local costs are easy to miss. Sacramento County charges a documentary transfer tax of $0.55 per $500 of value. Inside Sacramento city limits, the city adds its own real-property transfer tax of $2.75 per $1,000. Elk Grove, Citrus Heights, Rancho Cordova and Folsom are not under the city's tax, so confirm which line the address sits on and who the contract says will pay. If the house is worth less than the payoff, the lender has to approve a short sale, and those take longer, so start early.

If you sign a contract with an investor after a Notice of Default has been recorded on an owner-occupied house, California's Home Equity Sales Contract Act may give you a cancellation right until midnight of the fifth business day after you sign, or until 8 a.m. on the trustee-sale day, whichever comes first (Civil Code section 1695.4). A buyer covered by that law has to give you the notice in writing. Use the window. Walk away from anyone who wants the deed signed over before escrow, promises to "stop the foreclosure," or asks for an upfront fee.

Put the options on one page before you pick one

By the end of the first week you should be able to compare, in writing: a reinstatement figure and whether you can actually pay it; a listing estimate if you have enough time before the sale date; and an as-is cash price that can close in time to pay the lender directly. You can ask for a cash offer on the Sacramento house with the recorded notices in hand, then set that number next to the other two. A cash sale is the wrong move if a modification or repayment plan would let you stay, or if you have real equity and enough months left for a conventional listing. It is worth pricing when the sale date is close, the house needs work you cannot pay for before showings, or nobody can keep it ready for a market listing.
