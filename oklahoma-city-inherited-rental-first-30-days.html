You inherited an Oklahoma City rental with a tenant in it. What to do in the first 30 days

Most heirs find out about the tenant the same week they find out about the house. Rent is due on the first, the tenant wants to know who to pay, and nobody in the family has a key that works. The decisions you make in the first month don't lock you into selling or keeping the house, but a few of them are hard to undo. Here is the order we'd tackle them in.

Days 1 to 7: the lease didn't die with the landlord

Under Oklahoma's Residential Landlord and Tenant Act, a death changes who owns the house, not the tenant's right to stay. A fixed-term lease runs to its end date. A month-to-month tenant stays month to month. Don't text the tenant that they need to be out by the end of the month. That isn't a lawful notice and it tends to start the relationship badly.

What helps this week:

  • Find the lease, the last few rent receipts, and any record of the security deposit. Check the decedent's bank statements for rent deposits if the paperwork is missing.
  • Send the tenant a short, calm note: the owner has passed, the lease stays in effect, and you'll confirm in writing who should receive rent once someone has legal authority.
  • Call the insurer. A landlord (dwelling fire) policy in the decedent's name may need the estate added. Ask what it covers while title is in limbo.

Days 7 to 14: who is allowed to collect rent and sign anything?

Until someone has authority, nobody should sign a new lease, take a new deposit, or sign a sale contract. In Oklahoma that authority usually comes from one of three places: a trust that already owns the house (the successor trustee acts), a probate case where the court appoints a personal representative, or a recorded transfer-on-death deed that passes the house straight to the named beneficiary.

Two Oklahoma details trip families up. The $50,000 small-estate affidavit in 58 O.S. § 393 reaches money and personal property, not title to a house. And smaller estates, generally $200,000 or less, may qualify for summary administration under 58 O.S. § 245, which is still a court case but a shorter one. Days 14 to 21: get the deposit straight before it becomes a dispute

Oklahoma requires security deposits to sit in an escrow account at a federally insured institution in the state. When a landlord's interest ends, including by death or sale, 41 O.S. § 115 says whoever holds the deposit must either transfer it to the successor and tell the tenant in writing who that is, or refund it. In practice the deposit is often missing from the paperwork because the decedent kept it in a personal account. Write down the amount the lease says, look for it in the statements, and plan for it to show up as a credit to the buyer on any closing statement.

Days 21 to 30: keep it, list it, or sell it occupied

Now you have enough to compare real options instead of guesses.

  • Keep it as a rental. Makes sense if the house is in decent shape, the tenant pays, and one heir actually wants to be a landlord or pay a property manager.
  • List it vacant. Usually the highest gross price, but you have to wait for the lease to end or give at least 30 days' written notice on a month-to-month tenancy under 41 O.S. § 111, served in person or posted and also sent by certified mail. Then come cleanup, repairs, and showings.
  • Sell it with the tenant in place. A buyer who purchases occupied rentals takes over the lease and the deposit. No move-out, no showings around someone's work schedule, and a closing date that can wait for the probate order.

Whichever way you lean, get the numbers on paper side by side.
