Burst pipe in an Austin house? Three things to sort before you sell as-is

The drywall is cut to four feet, the floors are cupped, and an adjuster has already walked the house. Now you're staring at a ranch in North Austin off Burnet, a Crestview bungalow, or a place near Onion Creek, and the question isn't "should I paint." It's whether to keep paying the mortgage on a house you can't live in, or sell it as-is and move on.

This is for that moment — not for a small drip under a sink you already fixed. Three Austin-specific checks decide whether repairing, listing damaged, or taking cash leaves you with more money.

1. Water damage or flood? Texas makes you pick a box

The Texas Seller's Disclosure Notice (Property Code §5.008) treats these as different lines.

  • A supply line that failed in the attic, a frozen pipe that split during a hard freeze, or a water heater that dumped into the garage usually goes under "Water Damage Not Due to a Flood Event."
  • Water that came through the back door from Onion Creek, Williamson Creek, or a reservoir release goes in the flood section — flood insurance, prior natural flood events, 100-year or 500-year floodplain or floodway, NFIP claims, FEMA or SBA help.

Buyers read that form closely. Cash as-is does not erase the duty to fill it in (some fiduciary estate sales are exempt; ask a Texas attorney if that might apply). The notice is due on or before the contract's effective date. If the buyer gets it late, the statute generally lets them terminate within seven days of receiving it.

Don't paint over a stain and leave the line blank. Disclose what you know, and let the offer price it.

2. Who is holding the insurance check?

If there's a mortgage, your servicer often holds the claim money first. Texas Department of Insurance guidance is that when a loan is on the house, the insurer usually sends payment to the mortgage company, which has to contact you within about 10 days of getting it. You then work with the lender to release funds for repairs — often in stages as work is done. A gutted house and a half-spendable check is a common Austin story after a freeze or a big plumbing break.

Two other claim details change the sell-vs-repair math:

  • Replacement-cost policies often pay twice. The first check is typically the repair estimate minus depreciation. The second comes after repairs are finished. If you sell before repairing, ask the insurer in writing whether you'll ever see that second payment.
  • Deadlines run both ways. Keep the claim number, adjuster's estimate, and every letter from the servicer in one folder. A buyer who understands the held funds can price them; a retail buyer whose lender wants a finished house usually can't.

You can still sell with a pending claim. Title will ask who gets paid what at closing. Have the servicer's statement of held funds ready before you compare offers.

3. Floodplain and the City's 50% rule

Before you spend another dollar on a big rebuild, look up the address on the City of Austin's ATXFloodPro map. Austin sits in Flash Flood Alley; the City has said roughly 10% of its land is in the floodplain, and its regulatory floodplain has been tied to FEMA's 500-year map while Atlas 14 updates roll through. A house that "wasn't in the floodplain" a decade ago may be now.

The rule that catches sellers is substantial damage: if the house is in the floodplain and the cost to repair equals or exceeds 50% of the structure's value before the damage, repairs have to meet floodplain rules. Talk to the Floodplain Office before you start a rebuild that might trigger it. Document the pre-damage value and the repair estimate. Onion Creek owners have lived through buyout waves before; the paperwork trail matters whether you rebuild or sell.

Mold: license rules if you remediate, disclosure if you don't

You do not need a licensed mold company just to sell. If you hire one, Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation rules apply: visible mold under about 25 contiguous square feet can generally be handled without a license; above that, the assessor and remediator must be different licensed companies. A proper Certificate of Mold Damage Remediation helps a retail buyer and, under Insurance Code §544.303, can protect against certain adverse underwriting decisions when the underlying cause was fixed.

Selling as-is: disclose the water event and any mold you know about. Skip the certificate if you're not remediating — don't hide the damage.

One tax trap if the house was unlivable

If you kept a homestead exemption under Tax Code §11.135 while the house was uninhabitable from casualty or wind/water damage, selling before the repair or replacement is finished can trigger a tax equal to the exemption savings for each year, plus interest, with a lien at sale. Ask your title company to check early. A temporary disaster exemption under §11.35 (when one applies) has its own proration rules. Neither is a reason to panic; both are reasons to ask before you pick a closing date.

When cash usually beats waiting

Repair-and-list still wins when the damage is small, the claim covers full replacement, and you can manage contractors. Waiting gets expensive when the servicer is holding most of the money, the contractor's bid is higher than the adjuster's, you're paying a mortgage and utilities on an empty shell, or the 50% floodplain rule would force a bigger rebuild than you want.
