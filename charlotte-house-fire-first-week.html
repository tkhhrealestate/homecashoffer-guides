The week after a house fire in Charlotte: what to sort before you sell or rebuild

A typical Charlotte fire does not level the house. Charlotte Fire Department knocks down a kitchen grease fire in a 1960s brick ranch off Albemarle Road, or a space-heater fire in a mill house near NoDa, and everyone is out safely. What is left is often worse than it looks from the street: hose water in the red-clay crawl space, smoke in the ductwork, and a mortgage that does not pause while you stay with family in Matthews or Concord. The decisions that are hard to undo happen in the first week, before a restoration company has you sign an assignment of benefits and before the house has sat empty long enough to change the insurance.

Day one to three: secure it, notify the insurer, and register the boards

Tarping the roof and stopping further water intrusion is part of your duty under North Carolina's standard fire policy, the baseline written into G.S. 58-44-16. That same statute is also why you should give the insurer written notice quickly, not just a phone call, and start a simple inventory of what burned or was soaked.

If you board the house, Charlotte's city code requires you to register a boarded-up residential structure with the City within 48 hours. That registration lasts six months and cannot be renewed, so a burned house cannot stay plywooded and ignored. Keep the fire report, photos taken before any tear-out, and every letter from the insurer in one folder. You will be asked for all of it later, whether you rebuild or sell.

Watch the proof-of-loss window, and read the vacancy clause

The standard policy says the insured must give the insurer a sworn proof of loss within 60 days after the loss, unless the insurer extends that in writing. Get any extension in writing. The same standard form generally says the insurer is not liable for a later loss while the building is vacant or unoccupied beyond 60 consecutive days, unless the policy adds something different. If the house will sit empty while you decide, ask your agent about a vacancy endorsement or a vacant-dwelling policy now, not in month three.

Two other clocks are worth putting on a calendar. If you and the insurer cannot agree on the amount of the loss, the standard policy's appraisal clause lets either side demand appraisal in writing. And a lawsuit over a denied or underpaid claim generally must be started within three years after the inception of the loss, measured from the date of the damage, not the date of a later denial (G.S. 58-44-16). Talk to a North Carolina attorney well before that date if the claim is stuck.

Do not let a contractor invoice become a surprise lien

Board-up crews, water-extraction companies, and tear-out contractors often start work before the insurer has agreed on the scope. In North Carolina, a contractor or supplier can file a claim of lien with the Mecklenburg County Clerk of Superior Court, not the Register of Deeds, no later than 120 days after the last day it furnished labor or materials at the site (G.S. 44A-12). Because it is indexed under the owner's name at the Clerk, a quick look at recorded deeds will miss it, and it can surface in a title search when you go to sell.

Read anything that assigns benefits or directs the insurer to pay the contractor before you sign it. A full fire rebuild generally needs a licensed general contractor once the project costs $40,000 or more; you can look a license up with the North Carolina Licensing Board for General Contractors. When you do pay someone, get a final invoice and a signed lien waiver.

Then compare rebuild, list, and sell as-is, including who keeps the claim

Many Charlotte houses that look "just smoky" from the curb are a different project once firefighting water has sat in a crawl space. Red clay holds moisture, and rotted sill plates or mold can show up weeks after the floors looked dry. Structural, electrical, plumbing, mechanical, and roofing work generally needs permits from Mecklenburg County Code Enforcement through AccelaMeck. Many policies also pay actual cash value first and hold back the rest of replacement cost until repairs are finished, so selling before you rebuild can mean leaving that holdback on the table. Ask the adjuster for that number in writing before you sign a contract with anyone.

You can sell with the claim still open. The purchase contract has to say who keeps the money: you sell the house as it sits and keep collecting from the insurer, or you assign the remaining claim rights and the price reflects that. If there is a mortgage, the check is often payable to you and the lender, so tell a North Carolina closing attorney about the claim at the start. Most sellers also have to complete the state's Residential Property and Owners' Association Disclosure Statement; "No Representation" is an option on many questions, but the form itself warns that failing to disclose hidden defects can still lead to civil liability. A fire, and water that ran into the crawl space, belong on the form.

Cosmetic smoke damage in a house you still want, with insurance actually funding the work, is usually a reason to rebuild rather than take an as-is discount. An as-is sale is worth pricing when the damage is structural, the holdback is small, or you simply do not want to run a rebuild from someone else's guest room.
