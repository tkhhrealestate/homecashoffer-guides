Your Dallas tenant just moved out. A two-week checklist before you re-rent or sell

A tenant handing back keys is the one moment a small landlord gets to see the house empty, with nothing in the way. Maybe it's a 1950s pier-and-beam in Oak Cliff, a brick ranch in Pleasant Grove, or a 1980s house in Garland you bought when you lived nearby. The next two weeks decide a lot: whether you get sued over the deposit, how much the turnover really costs, and whether you put the house back on the rental market or finally sell it. Here is the order we would work through it.

Days 1 to 3: walk-through, photos, and the forwarding address

Do the move-out inspection as soon as you can, and photograph or video every room, including inside cabinets, the water heater closet, and the condenser outside. Compare it with your move-in condition form if you have one. Note the meter readings and switch the utilities to your name so the house isn't dark or dry while you decide.

Then make sure you have the tenant's forwarding address in writing. Under Texas Property Code section 92.107, you are not obligated to return the deposit or send the itemized list until the tenant gives you a written statement of their forwarding address. Once you have it, the clock in section 92.103 generally gives you until the 30th day after the tenant surrendered the premises to refund the deposit, less lawful deductions, with a written description and itemized list of those deductions under section 92.104. You can't deduct for normal wear and tear.

Take this part seriously. Under section 92.109, a landlord who keeps a deposit in bad faith can owe $100, three times the amount wrongfully withheld, and the tenant's attorney's fees. A landlord who fails to return the deposit or provide the itemized list within the 30 days is presumed to have acted in bad faith. Calendar the deadline the day you get the forwarding address.

Days 3 to 7: price the turnover honestly

Get real bids, not guesses, for what it takes to rent the house again. In Dallas the expensive surprises tend to be the same few items:

  • HVAC. An older system that limped through the last summer often fails during the next one. Ask the technician for the age and refrigerant type and whether replacement is likely soon.
  • Foundation and plumbing. Expansive clay soil across much of Dallas County moves with wet and dry seasons. Sticking doors, new drywall cracks, or a slab leak are worth a professional look before you sign a new lease.
  • Roof. North Texas hail claims are common. Check whether earlier damage was ever repaired and whether your landlord policy has a separate wind and hail deductible.
  • Make-ready. Paint, flooring, appliances, rekeying (Texas requires rekeying within seven days after a tenant turnover under section 92.156), and cleaning.

Add the vacancy itself: mortgage, insurance, Dallas County property taxes on a non-homestead house, utilities, and lawn care for however many weeks the house sits empty plus a realistic leasing period. That total is what re-renting actually costs, before the first new rent check.

Days 7 to 10: the paperwork that changes when you sell

If selling is on the table, a few items look different once the house is no longer a rental.

  • Property taxes. Dallas Central Appraisal District values the property, and the Dallas County Tax Office collects. Texas property taxes are generally due by January 31 and become delinquent on February 1, when penalty and interest start. A closing will prorate the current year, so ask the title company how they are handling the year's bill.
  • City of Dallas rental registration. Dallas has a rental registration program for many single-family rentals. If yours is registered, update or close out that registration after you sell so notices don't keep coming to you.
  • Insurance. Many landlord policies limit coverage once a house has been vacant for a set number of days. Tell the insurer it's vacant and ask what still applies, especially going into a winter freeze.
  • Federal taxes. Texas has no state income tax, but the federal side matters. A rental doesn't qualify for the home-sale exclusion the way a main home can, and depreciation you took (or could have taken) is generally recaptured. A 1031 exchange into another property is an option if you want to stay invested. Talk to a CPA before you sign, not after.

Texas has no state transfer tax, and in Dallas the seller commonly pays for the owner's title policy, though everything is negotiable in the contract. If the house came to you through an estate rather than a purchase, check whether title was ever cleared. The probate timeline by state shows how Texas compares with other states.

Days 10 to 14: re-rent, list, or sell as-is

With the deposit handled and real numbers in hand, the choice is clearer.

  • Re-rent if the turnover is mostly cosmetic, the big systems have years left, and you still want to be a landlord, including the late-night maintenance calls.
  • Renovate and list if the house will show well after a reasonable make-ready, you can carry it for the time it takes to sell, and you are fine handling buyer inspection and repair requests.
  • Sell as-is for cash if the bids came back high, you live out of state, you're tired of managing it, or you want one closing date and no more turnovers.
