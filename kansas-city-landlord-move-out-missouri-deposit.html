Kansas City landlord after a move-out: the Missouri deposit rules, then the keep-or-sell math

Plenty of Kansas City landlords own one or two houses they never planned to be landlords on: a parent's bungalow in Waldo, a starter home in Raytown, a split-level in the Northland that became a rental when the family moved. When the tenant leaves, you have a short, legally timed window to close out the lease properly and a longer, money-driven decision about the house. This is the order we would handle both on the Missouri side of the state line.

The move-out inspection: give notice, then document

Missouri's security-deposit statute, RSMo section 535.300, requires the landlord to give the tenant reasonable notice of the date and time of the move-out inspection, and the tenant has the right to be present. Send that notice in writing, then photograph and video every room, the furnace and water heater, the roof line and the yard. Read the meters and transfer utilities into your name so the house doesn't freeze in a January vacancy.

The 30-day deposit clock

Under the same statute, the landlord has 30 days after the tenancy ends to return the deposit or send a written, itemized list of damages and charges withheld. Allowed deductions include unpaid rent, damage beyond ordinary wear and tear, and certain costs from a tenant's failure to give proper notice. The statute also caps a deposit at two months' rent, which is worth checking if you inherited the lease from someone else.

The penalty is the reason to calendar it: a tenant can recover up to twice the amount wrongfully withheld. Keep receipts for every repair you deduct, and don't deduct for faded paint or normal carpet wear.

Price the turnover honestly

Before you decide anything, write down what it costs to make the house rentable again: paint, flooring, appliance repair, cleaning, landscaping, and the rent you lose each month it sits empty. Add the carrying costs that keep running while it is vacant: mortgage, Jackson County (or Clay or Platte County) property taxes, insurance, and utilities. Ask your insurer whether a vacant house changes your policy; many landlord policies restrict coverage after a set number of days of vacancy.

Re-rent, list, or sell as-is

  • Re-rent if the turnover is light, the rent still covers costs with room to spare, and you're comfortable doing it again in a year or two.
  • List with an agent if the house shows well empty and you can carry it for the 60 to 90 days a listing and closing usually take.
  • Sell as-is for cash if the turnover is heavy (old roof, aging furnace, foundation movement), if you live out of state, or if you simply want out without another round of repairs and showings.

Taxes worth asking about before you sell

A rental is taxed differently from a home you live in. Depreciation you claimed (or were entitled to claim) is generally recaptured when you sell, and the home-sale exclusion usually doesn't apply to a house that was a rental for most of the past five years. Some landlords look at a 1031 exchange into another property instead. Have a tax professional run the numbers before you sign anything, so the net you compare is the net after tax.
